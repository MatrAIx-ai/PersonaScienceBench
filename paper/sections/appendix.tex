\section{Full Profiles and Prompt Templates}
\label{app:profiles}

Profiles are complete rendered MatrAIx persona records \citep{li2026matraix}
injected verbatim into the researcher simulator's system identity, with no
truncation, summarization, or projection onto the task domain. Selection had two
stages: an original 300-profile sample was rescreened to 180 eligible records
from which a source-balanced, behaviorally diverse 30-profile cohort was drawn
without reference to model scores; ten profiles were then selected by largest
absolute deviation from the persona-off baseline in the exploratory pilot, in
ranked order 046, 003, 004, 089, 001, 127, 262, 224, 148, and 026. Persona 155 is
retained as an additional condition outside that set. The persona-off control
uses the same simulator, protocol, and general-knowledge access with the identity
block omitted.

\section{Implementation and Reproducibility}
\label{app:implementation}

The main campaign uses \texttt{gpt-6-astra} via the direct OpenAI Responses API
at reasoning effort \texttt{low} for the actor, the researcher simulator, and the
configured model judge; reasoning effort \texttt{none} is unsupported by this
model and \texttt{low} was selected explicitly. Native non-model evaluators are
retained wherever a source provides one. The exploratory 30-profile pilot used
\texttt{GPT-6 Luna} at reasoning effort \texttt{none} and is not pooled with the
main campaign.

Each trial requests 128\,GB RAM, 30 CPUs, and a 12-hour Slurm allocation, with up
to 30 concurrent episode processes and BLAS/OpenMP thread counts pinned to one.
Protocol limits are twelve researcher requests and sixty actor calls per episode
in the execution and design tracks, and up to ten question turns plus a final
attempt in the SciConvBench track.

Initial three-condition trials were submitted as Slurm jobs 1957983 (trial~1),
1962301 (trial~2), and 1962305 (trial~3); trial~1 processed 600 episodes in
3\,h\,21\,min\,22\,s with 536 scored, 32 missing submissions, and 32 technical
errors, and retained three episodes from an earlier interrupted campaign. A
cancelled seven-condition job (1957850) is not an additional trial. The
nine-profile expansion was submitted as sequential 600-episode batches, jobs
1964501 through 1964513, chained with \texttt{afterany} dependencies after
resumed trial~3 (job 1964348), grouped as [046, 003, 004], [089, 001, 127], and
[262, 224, 026] across trials. An \texttt{afterany} dependency enforces
non-overlap but does not certify successful completion.

\section{Extended Outcomes and Statistical Details}
\label{app:statistics}

Matched-task analysis includes a task only when every compared condition produced
an evaluable result. In trial~1 this retained 185 of 200 tasks---31 ASI, 40 Bix,
34 Discovery, 40 EXP, and 40 SciConv---with super-family counts of 46 life, 57
physical and engineering, 54 mathematics and computer science, and 28 social
sciences and humanities. These are per-trial analysis counts, not replacement
sampling quotas.

Independent repeats are averaged within each task--condition cell before
aggregation; paired baselines are retained; super-family summaries are
task-weighted. Because multiple tasks may derive from a single source study,
uncertainty estimation is source-study aware rather than treating tasks as
independent.

\section{Behavioral Metrics and Annotation Rubric}
\label{app:behavior}

Metric definitions separate mandatory from voluntary activity, and researcher
activity from actor activity. Plan and result handoffs are mandatory in this
interface and are not counted as engagement; extra review requests measure
simulator activity, while questions and new proposals inside actor handoffs
measure actor initiative. The SciConvBench runner exports clarification recall
and precision, final resolution rate, conversationally grounded resolution rate,
silent resolution rate, memory consistency, and intent capture, where final
resolution decomposes into grounded and silent components. Visible-message length
is measured in characters by role rather than whitespace words, to remain valid
across communication languages, and is kept distinct from API token usage. The
actor's first SciConvBench question precedes any persona-conditioned reply, so
persona-attributable activity is measured only after the first researcher
response.

\section{Extended Positioning and Validity}
\label{app:related-validity}

Interactive benchmarks establish that agent performance depends on information
exchange, but most use one cooperative user policy. Work on non-collaborative
simulators and evolved persona policies shows that impatience, digression,
incomplete disclosure, and other user behaviors can alter measured performance
\citep{shim2025noncollaborative,chopra2026ppol}. Those evaluations primarily
target transactional domains. \benchmark instead preserves scientific
deliverables and native verifiers, making it possible to distinguish a change
in conversational behavior from a change in scientific correctness.

Persona-conditioned simulation also raises representativeness concerns. Prior
work finds substantial flexibility in persona construction and evaluation, and
grounded and ungrounded synthetic users need not surface the same concerns as
real experts
\citep{batzner2025whosepersonae,cummins2025analyticflexibility,nguyen2026sycamore}.
We therefore use profiles as controlled interaction conditions, not as
substitutes for real researchers or demographic groups. Complete profiles vary
several attributes together, so this design estimates effects of whole
collaborator conditions rather than effects of individual traits.

Following fitness-for-purpose accounts of simulated-user evaluation, we
separate persona fidelity, behavioral validity, task validity, and
distributional validity \citep{liu2026fitnessforpurpose,li2026humansimulation}.
Upstream adherence tests provide evidence that MatrAIx profiles affect declared
behavior \citep{li2026matraix}; our benchmark tests whether those controlled
differences expose changes in scientific-agent behavior and native task
outcomes. It does not establish that traces match real scientist behavior or
that the outcome-selected profile panel represents a researcher population.

A further class of threats concerns the simulator rather than the profile, and
each bounds a different assumption of our design. LLM user simulators drift
toward assistant behavior instead of sustaining an assigned role
\citep{jeong2026assistantbias}, which for a researcher required to review and
withhold approval would compress persona conditions toward the cooperative
control. Constraining what a simulated user knows is also harder than a prompt
instruction implies \citep{balog2026bounding}; our configuration grants general
knowledge in every condition, so a profile alters disposition and disclosure
behavior rather than enforcing an epistemic boundary, and results do not
measure the consequences of a researcher genuinely lacking a fact. Models can
also perform worse when an equivalent task is distributed across multiple
turns rather than posed in one turn \citep{laban2025lost}, so a profile that
provokes more review rounds may depress outcomes partly through conversation
length; exchange counts are therefore reported alongside outcomes, although
this design does not fully separate the two mechanisms. Finally, deference is
distinct from under-asking:
an agent that accepts a researcher's incorrect claim fails scientifically while
appearing cooperative, paralleling documented overreliance on unreliable tools
\citep{yang2026overreliance}. Because we do not plant false researcher claims,
evidence-versus-claim judgment is outside the present scope.
