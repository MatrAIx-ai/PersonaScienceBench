\section{Limitations and Responsible Use}
\label{sec:limitations}

\paragraph{Simulated collaborators, not human scientists.}
Every researcher in this benchmark is a language model conditioned on a
profile. This establishes at most that agents are sensitive to
\emph{simulated} collaborator variation. Persona-conditioned simulation
distinguishes several forms of validity that do not imply one another
\citep{liu2026fitnessforpurpose}: we rely on upstream persona-adherence
evidence for persona fidelity \citep{li2026matraix}, contribute evidence about
task validity in a scientific domain, and claim neither behavioral validity
against real scientist behavior nor distributional validity over any
researcher population. Establishing that simulated researchers elicit the
same agent failures real collaborators would elicit requires human comparison
data that this study does not collect. Evidence in a neighboring scientific
domain is a direct warning: synthetic personas evaluating a genomics
visualization system drifted toward concerns real experts never raised and
missed a preference the expert study observed \citep{nguyen2026sycamore}.

\paragraph{A small, outcome-selected persona panel.}
The final ten profiles were chosen by largest absolute deviation from the
persona-off baseline in an exploratory pilot. This is an outcome-dependent
selection, and its consequences are specific: the panel characterizes the
range of collaborator effects rather than estimating a population average,
pilot effect sizes are inflated by selection and are reported as a selection
stage rather than as effect estimates, and transfer to the expanded task panel
is an empirical question rather than an assumption. Pilot and expanded panels
may overlap in tasks, and that overlap is disclosed rather than assumed away.
Ten profiles plus one control cannot support a factorial model over persona
attributes.

\paragraph{Full-profile confounding.}
Complete profiles are injected verbatim, so communication style, availability,
domain knowledge, methodological familiarity, and language vary together within
a single condition. A score difference between two personas therefore cannot be
attributed to any one dimension. This is a deliberate trade: full profiles
preserve the internal coherence of a collaborator and avoid hand-authored trait
prompts, at the cost of dimension-level attribution. The underlying schema
exposes these dimensions separately \citep{li2026matraix}, so single-dimension
manipulation is feasible in principle and is the natural next experiment; it is
not performed here. Relatedly, some profiles contain internally inconsistent
attributes, so behavior must be read against the exact injected text rather
than a coherent imagined persona.

\paragraph{Persona and user state are not the same thing.}
A profile supplies persistent disposition, not knowledge of the specific study
under analysis, and many collaboration-relevant behaviors depend on a
changeable user state including current goals, accumulated context, trust, and
frustration \citep{liu2026fitnessforpurpose}. Study-specific context is
task-owned and held identical across conditions by design, which is what makes
score differences interpretable, but it also means this benchmark does not
model a researcher whose knowledge of their own experiment is partial or
mistaken. The separation is also imperfect: rendered profiles include a small
interaction-state group, including a field declaring the holder's expertise gap
relative to the system, so a limited amount of state is bundled into each
persona condition rather than held fixed. Whether persona conditioning persists
undiminished across a long
multi-round episode is not measured here, and drift within an episode would
weaken the manipulation rather than the finding.

\paragraph{Simulator-side threats to the manipulation.}
Three findings about LLM user simulators bound what our persona conditions can
be assumed to hold fixed. First, simulators drift toward assistant behavior
rather than sustaining an assigned user role
\citep{jeong2026assistantbias}; our researcher is required to review, withhold
approval, and request revision, so any such drift would compress the difference
between persona conditions and the persona-off control in the direction of
helpfulness. Second, bounding a simulated user's knowledge is harder than a
prompt instruction implies \citep{balog2026bounding}. Our configuration grants
the simulator general knowledge in all conditions, so it does not claim to
implement a knowledge-bounded researcher; what a persona changes is disposition
and disclosure behavior, not an enforced epistemic boundary, and results should
not be read as measuring the consequences of a researcher genuinely not knowing
something. Third, models can perform worse when an equivalent task is
distributed across multiple turns rather than posed in one turn
\citep{laban2025lost}, so a persona that induces more review rounds may depress
outcomes partly through conversation length rather than through its stated
traits; exchange counts are reported alongside outcomes so that this
alternative explanation remains visible, but the present design does not
separate the two. A related risk is deference: an agent may accept a
researcher's incorrect claim rather than checking it against data evidence, a
failure mode analogous to documented overreliance on unreliable tools
\citep{yang2026overreliance}. We do not systematically plant false researcher
claims, so this benchmark does not measure evidence-versus-claim judgment, and
that remains a natural extension.

\paragraph{Metric caveats inherited from sources.}
Native metrics carry documented irregularities that we propagate rather than
silently smooth. In the SciConvBench track the conversation-grounded resolution
rate is the source's primary success metric, and final resolution decomposes
definitionally into grounded and silent resolution; however the published
per-domain tables do not satisfy that identity exactly, so components are
reported as given and never reconstructed arithmetically from one another
\citep{somasekharan2026sciconvbench}. DiscoveryBench scores the hypothesis text
and does not score the analysis workflow, so a persona effect on that source
concerns stated conclusions rather than demonstrated analytical soundness
\citep{majumder2024discoverybench}. Because our EXP-Bench conversion is
design-only, its numbers are not comparable to native EXP-Bench results
\citep{kon2025expbench}, and because the BixBench dataset was revised after
publication, our numbers are not comparable to the scores in its paper
\citep{mitchener2025bixbench}. Metrics normalized to a common 0--100 range
across sources are not a common calibrated measure and are not pooled as one.

\paragraph{Language and domain heterogeneity.}
Profiles differ in communication language, and at least one selected profile
communicates in Mandarin while others use English. Language therefore covaries
with profile identity, and metrics sensitive to text volume are affected;
visible-message length is reported in characters rather than whitespace words
for this reason, but language remains a confound rather than a controlled
factor. Domain background likewise varies across profiles in ways that
correlate with the field composition of the task panel.

\paragraph{Benchmark and field confounding.}
Equal per-source quotas produce an unequal and non-orthogonal field
distribution. All selected social-science and humanities tasks originate from
DiscoveryBench and BixBench dominates the life sciences, so a field effect and
a source effect cannot be fully separated. Task format is similarly entangled
with source: design-only, dialogue, and execution tracks each come from a single
benchmark. Comparisons are therefore reported within source and format where
coverage permits, and cross-field claims are correspondingly weaker. Because
multiple tasks may derive from one source study, tasks are not statistically
independent, and uncertainty estimates must account for source-study clustering.

\paragraph{Three repeats and grading uncertainty.}
Three independent episodes per task--condition cell bound how precisely
within-condition variation can be estimated, particularly for
high-variance tasks and for detecting small effects. Where a source benchmark
requires a model judge, scores inherit that judge's biases and prompt
sensitivity; EXP-Bench's three judge repetitions reduce grading noise but are
not independent trials and must not be counted as such. Native non-model
evaluators are used wherever available precisely to limit this exposure.
Semantic annotations used in case studies are judgments made with outcome
scores hidden from the annotator and a subset checked by a second judge, but
they remain interpretive evidence rather than measurement.

\paragraph{Technical missingness and single actor.}
Episodes fail for infrastructure reasons---API errors, truncation, evaluator
incompatibility---and these are reported separately from substantive failures
such as missing submissions. Matched-task analysis excludes any task with a
technical error in any compared condition, which protects pairing but changes
the effective panel and can itself correlate with condition difficulty.
Sensitivity of conclusions to these exclusions is reported. The completed
campaign evaluates a single actor model, so no claim is made about whether
other agents exhibit the same collaborator sensitivity or the same ordering
across personas; the actor comparison remains an unexecuted proposal.

\paragraph{Provenance, licensing, and privacy.}
Researcher profiles derive from the MatrAIx persona corpus
\citep{li2026matraix}, whose human-grounded records are extracted from public
sources and de-identified by removing direct identifiers. Exact injected
prompts, profile hashes, and renderer versions are published so that
conditions are reproducible. Private evaluator references, hidden graders, and
credentials are kept outside actor and simulator access and are not released.

\paragraph{Interpretation of persona attributes.}
These profiles do not represent real people, and no attribute should be read as
a property of any demographic group. The upstream release is explicit that
human-grounded records are not population-representative, that calibration
targets only four marginal distributions rather than joint coverage over all
1,290 schema dimensions, and that human studies remain necessary before
applying conclusions to real populations \citep{li2026matraix}; broader reviews
of persona practice make the same point about representativeness and
transparency \citep{batzner2025whosepersonae,cummins2025analyticflexibility}.
Results here describe how an agent behaved with a \emph{simulated} collaborator
carrying certain stated attributes. They are not evidence about how such an
agent would serve, or fail, any real population of scientists, and should not be
used to justify claims about groups of researchers or to substitute for
evaluation with human collaborators in consequential deployment decisions.
